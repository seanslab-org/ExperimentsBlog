The more I work with AI, the less I want to work with people. That worries me.

I spend much of my working day with AI agents, writing code and handling marketing and operations. They are fast, knowledgeable, and capable. I can move from a question to a working result without finding someone who has time to help. That freedom is useful. I have become heavily dependent on it.

But sometimes an agent repeats the same mistake after I have explained it several times. It says it understands, then does it again. I get frustrated. Then angry.

Venting at the AI does not help. Sometimes I carry that frustration into conversations with my family or colleagues. The machine is where the anger starts; a person ends up receiving it.

I wrote about this in October because I wanted to know whether other people recognized the pattern. I still do not have a neat answer. But I think it deserves a place in the conversation about AI productivity.

## The difficult part is that it works

If these tools were simply useless, walking away would be easier.

They help me get real work done. That is what makes the relationship complicated. I can be impressed by the output and unhappy with how I feel at the end of the day. I can want to close the session and still want to ask one more question.

When I describe the problem as a bad answer, I leave out most of it. A bad answer is something I can correct. The harder question is what repeated correction does to my patience—and what happens to that patience when I leave the desk.

There is also a change in my expectations. When a tool can respond immediately and attempt almost anything, ordinary collaboration can start to feel slow. People need context. They have other priorities. They disagree. I have noticed myself becoming less willing to do that work with them.

That is a change in me worth watching, even on days when the software I produce is better.

## An apology is not a repair

An AI conversation uses familiar social language: “You're right.” “I understand.” “I'll fix it.”

I find it easy to hear those words as a commitment. Then the same failure returns, and it feels more aggravating than a plain error message. I have already explained this. We have already been through this.

But a conversational acknowledgment does not establish that the underlying problem has been fixed. The useful evidence is a changed result: the right file, the passing check, the behavior I asked for.

This distinction gives me a practical place to intervene. Instead of explaining the same thing with more force, I can ask what evidence would show that the next attempt is different. If there is no new evidence, another round may just repeat the frustration.

That is a workflow question I can act on. Getting angrier is not a verification method.

## The people around me did not cause the failed task

The part I am least comfortable admitting is the spillover.

It is tempting to explain a short temper by describing the session that caused it. The agent wasted time. It ignored instructions. It broke something that was working.

Those explanations do not make the people around me responsible for absorbing the result. If I take that anger into a family conversation, that is mine to address.

I do not want to turn this into a dramatic claim about what AI does to everyone. This is my experience. I have also noticed that getting enough rest seems to help. That observation is useful to me, but it is not a complete explanation or a proven remedy.

What I want is a way of working that leaves room for a decent conversation after the work ends.

## A few experiments worth trying

I cannot present the following as habits I have mastered. They are practical experiments suggested by the pattern I have noticed.

**Make a repeated failure a decision point.** When the same correction comes back, stop and choose: narrow the task, inspect the evidence, try another approach, or leave it for later. More attempts should have a reason beyond wanting the last attempt to have worked.

**Save the state before stepping away.** Write down what was requested, what failed, and the next thing to check. A pause is easier to take if returning does not require rebuilding the whole problem from memory.

**Check the result before accepting the reassurance.** “Fixed” is a claim. For code, a relevant test or a direct check of the behavior gives it meaning. For writing or operations, compare the actual result with the request. Keep the check small enough to do.

**Leave a gap before moving into a conversation.** Closing a laptop does not necessarily end the mood that formed in front of it. A short walk or a few quiet minutes is an experiment I can try before expecting myself to be fully present with someone else.

**Notice the human outcome as well as the task outcome.** Did the work get done? Did I finish with enough attention left for the people around me? These are separate questions. I need to ask both.

None of this requires treating the AI as an enemy, a colleague with bad intentions, or a substitute for human relationships. It requires noticing what is happening during the work and deciding when to change course.

## A design question for agent builders

There is a product question here too.

Could an agent make it easier to stop? Could it summarize failed attempts, preserve a clean checkpoint, and make the next step clear without another long exchange? Could it show what changed since the last failure instead of offering another confident apology?

I do not know whether those features would reduce frustration in practice. They would need to be tested. But they seem worth investigating alongside speed and task completion.

A workflow has an end, even when the chat box is still available. Helping someone reach that end may be a useful part of building an agent.

## The question I am left with

I want to keep the capability these tools give me. I also want to keep my patience, my willingness to work with people, and my attention to the people I care about.

I have not solved that balance. Writing it down is a way to be more honest about the cost instead of counting only the output.

If you work with AI agents for hours at a time, do you recognize this pattern? What actually helps you leave the frustration at the desk?
